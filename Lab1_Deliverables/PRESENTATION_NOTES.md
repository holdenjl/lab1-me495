## Questions to be ready for

**Does a thicker shaft mean more damping?** No. In this model it increases stiffness. A flywheel dimension changes inertia. An added dissipative device is needed to prescribe extra damping.

**Why change only one flywheel?** A common change to both inertias largely shifts resonance. The separate-wheel screen finds useful peak attenuation that the common-size study hides.

**Did you just move the peak?** Each new peak was located and its height and surrounding one-hertz cost checked. The design plots show the shifted peaks, including option 3 near 50 Hz.

**Does every option reduce vehicle vibration by 30%?** These are nominal bench-model predictions for the stated voltage input and relative wheel-speed metric. They are not modified-hardware tests or passenger-comfort measurements.

**Why prefer option 2?** It retains modeled steady speed and stays near the existing test range. The resistor is simpler to trial but reduces speed. The smaller motor-side wheel needs a much wider validation sweep. The oil damper creates continuous drag.

**What about flexible joints?** An alternate interpretation of the stiffness fit predicts smaller switching improvements for the geometry options, still nominally above 30%. Re-measure stiffness after assembly; that scenario is not a guaranteed bound.

**What exactly is option 4?** A rotating steel disk in a stationary sealed oil casing, with one 1 mm fluid gap on each face. Dimensions and fluid properties give an initial drag estimate. Seals, edge drag, added adapters and temperature need actual torque-speed testing.
